# Lang Basic Mathematics: Lesson Design Doc (Ch 1–4)

Nov 5, 2026 · @Emu

This doc changes chapters 1–4 of Lang’s *Basic Mathematics* into 19 short lessons. Each lesson has 10–14 screens, made from six screen types and six widgets. Lesson 1.2 has a design for each screen, with mockups in Paper (file “Basic Mathematics Lang”, page Screens). The other 18 lessons have step outlines.

## Who this is for and how lessons behave

The learner is one adult (you) who completed high school mathematics many years ago. The learner wants to learn mathematics again from the start. Arithmetic is slow but correct. Symbols and proofs are new.

Each lesson obeys these rules:

- **Words before symbols.** Use plain words first. Show each new symbol with its spoken form, for example “−*a*: say minus *a*”.
- **Puzzle first.** Give the learner a task with the idea first. Then show Lang’s rule or definition.
- **No pressure.** Do not use timers, lives or streak penalties. Show progress with a bar and a rule book.
- **Useful errors.** Give each likely mistake its own reply. The reply tells what is correct, names the error, and asks one question.
- **Lang’s structure.** Give the book section for each lesson. Keep Lang’s rule names (N1–N12, IN 1–IN 3, Theorems 1–4), so the learner can read the book at the same time.
- **Step-by-step proofs.** The learner gives the reason for each line of Lang’s proof. The learner never starts from an empty page.
- **Original practice.** Write new problems that teach Lang’s topics. Do not copy his text or exercises.
- **Short sessions.** Keep each lesson to 8–12 minutes. Start each lesson with one recall question about the previous rule.

## Lesson anatomy

All lessons use the same seven steps, so the learner always knows the position in the lesson. Each lesson has 10–14 screens in total.

1. **Recall** (1 screen). Ask one question about the rule from the previous lesson.
2. **Hook** (1–2). Show a concrete puzzle. The learner makes a guess before a calculation. Template: M1 Hook.
3. **Explore** (2–4). Use one widget with one part to drag. Add a “Try” task that shows the pattern. Template: M2 and D2 Explore.
4. **Name it** (1–2). Show Lang’s rule on a rule card with its name, a picture and its spoken form. Template: M3 Name it.
5. **Prove it** (1–3). Show Lang’s proof in the proof stepper, one line at a time. The learner taps the reason for each line. If Lang gives no proof, skip this step. Template: M4 and D4 Prove it.
6. **Practice** (3–4). Give problems that increase in difficulty: a direct use, a variation, a word problem, and a mix with an earlier rule. Template: M5 Practice.
7. **Trap and summary** (1). Ask one true-or-false question about the usual mistake. Then save the new rules to the rule book. Template: M6 Summary.

**Feedback rules**

- After feedback, the Check button changes to Continue. For a correct answer, show one line that tells why the answer is correct.
- If a wrong answer matches a known mistake, show the reply for that mistake. The reply tells what is correct, names the error, and asks one question.
- If a wrong answer does not match a known mistake, show the general hint for the lesson.
- Give a maximum of two hints. Then “Show me” gives the full solution and a similar problem. The lesson then ends on a correct answer.
- Do not use colour alone to show an error. The words must also give the meaning.

**Proof support**

- Show each proof line with its expression and an empty reason slot. The reason tray holds only rules from earlier lessons.
- If the learner selects the wrong reason, tell what that rule does. Example: “Commutativity changes the order. What changed in this line?”
- Where Lang writes “we leave the proof to the reader”, make a Prove it screen. Examples: N9, N10 and the cases of Theorem 1.

**Rule book**

The rule book keeps each rule and theorem that the learner earns, with its picture and one example. Later proofs use the rule book as the reason tray. Review sessions also use it. Each entry shows if Lang assumes the rule or proves it (see Assumed or proved).

## Chapter 1: Numbers

Chapter 1 makes the rule book for all later chapters. Lesson 1.2 is the pilot and has a design for each screen. The other lessons give their steps in the order of the lesson anatomy.

### 1.1 Numbers on a line (§1, 8 min)

**Goal:** put an integer on the line, and say −*n* as “minus *n*”. **Needs:** counting only.

1. Hook: a ruler starts at 0. Drag a point to “2 below zero”.
2. Explore: the line turns vertical and becomes a thermometer. Then the line turns horizontal and becomes a timeline. In Lang’s convention, the year −500 is 500 BC.
3. Name it: natural numbers (0, 1, 2, …), the origin, negative integers and integers.
4. Practice: put −4, 0 and 3 on the line. Find which number is farther from 0, −7 or 5. A cave is 30 m below sea level and a hill is 45 m above it. Find the distance between them (75 m).
5. Trap: “−7 is larger than −2, because 7 is larger than 2” (false).

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Puts −4 at 4 | Minus numbers are on the other side of 0. Which side is that? |
| Says −7 > −2 | −7 has the larger digit. Find −7 on the line. A point farther to the left is smaller. |

### 1.3 Minus times minus (§3, 12 min)

**Goal:** see that (−1)(−1) = 1 follows from earlier rules. Nobody invented this rule. **Needs:** 1.2, N1–N5.

1. Recall: −(−5) = ?
2. Hook: “Minus times minus is plus.” Why? Options: a person invented the rule; the rule follows from other rules; I do not know. Do not give the answer yet.
3. Explore (area model): turn a 3 × 4 rectangle to get 4 × 3 (commutativity). Divide 3 × (4 + 2) into 3 × 4 and 3 × 2 (distributivity).
4. Name it: commutativity, associativity, N6 (1*a* = *a*, 0*a* = 0) and distributivity. Lang assumes these rules. He assumes distributivity on one side only.
5. Prove it: (*b* + *c*)*a* = *ba* + *ca*. Use distributivity on the left side and commutativity, as Lang does.
6. Prove it: 0*a* = 0. Use Lang’s proof with 0*a* + *a* = (0 + 1)*a*.
7. Prove it: N7, (−1)*a* = −*a*.
8. Prove it: N8, −(*ab*) = (−*a*)*b*.
9. Prove it: N9, −(*ab*) = *a*(−*b*). Lang leaves this proof to the reader.
10. Result: (−1)(−1) = −(1 · (−1)) = −(−1) = 1. Reasons: N8, N6, N4. Then the learner proves N10, (−*a*)(−*b*) = *ab*, which Lang also leaves to the reader. Go back to the hook question.
11. Practice: (−3)(4); (−2)(−5); −2(3*a* − 4*b*) = −6*a* + 8*b*; justify each step of (2*a*)(−3*b*)(5*c*) = −30*abc*.
12. Trap: (−3)² = −9 (false, the result is 9). Save N6–N10.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| (−2)(−5) = −10 | There are two minus signs. What does the result screen show for (−1)(−1)? |
| −2(3*a* − 4*b*) = −6*a* − 8*b* | The −2 also multiplies −4*b*. What is (−2)(−4)? |

### 1.4 Powers and three famous formulas (§3, 12 min)

**Goal:** count factors to find N11 and N12, and see (*a* + *b*)² as a picture. **Needs:** 1.3.

1. Recall: (−1)(−1) = ?
2. Hook: a town has 50,000 people. The population doubles every 15 years. Guess the population after 45 years.
3. Explore: a doubling strip, 50,000 → 100,000 → 200,000 → 400,000, written as 2³ · 50,000.
4. Name it: *a*ⁿ (“*a* to the *n*”). N11: *a*ᵐ⁺ⁿ = *a*ᵐ*a*ⁿ, found by counting factors. N12: (*a*ᵐ)ⁿ = *a*ᵐⁿ. Also (*ab*)ⁿ = *a*ⁿ*b*ⁿ.
5. Explore (square tiles): divide a square with side *a* + *b* into *a*², two *ab* strips and *b*².
6. Prove it: (*a* + *b*)² = *a*² + 2*ab* + *b*² with distributivity. Then do (*a* − *b*)² and (*a* + *b*)(*a* − *b*). In the last product, the middle terms cancel.
7. Practice: 2³ · 2⁴; (*x* + 4)²; (2*x* − 3)²; (5 + *y*)(5 − *y*); expand (*x* + 1)(*x* − 2)(*x* + 3) = *x*³ + 2*x*² − 5*x* − 6.
8. Trap: (*a* + *b*)² = *a*² + *b*² (false). Save N11, N12 and the three formulas. Lang tells the reader to say the formulas aloud, like a poem.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| 2³ · 2⁴ = 2¹² | Count the 2s: three, then four more. What is the total? |
| (*x* + 4)² = *x*² + 16 | You have the two squares. The picture also has two strips, each with an area of 4*x*. |
| (2*x* − 3)² = 4*x*² + 12*x* + 9 | The middle term is 2 · (2*x*) · (−3). What sign does this term have? |

### 1.5 Even, odd and divisibility (§4, 10 min)

**Goal:** write even and odd numbers as 2*n* and 2*n* + 1, and prove Theorems 1 and 2. **Needs:** 1.4.

1. Recall: expand (*x* + 2)².
2. Hook: add three pairs of odd numbers. Find the pattern in the answers.
3. Explore (area model, width 2): show an even number as a 2 × *n* block. Show an odd number as the same block plus 1 square.
4. Name it: even = 2*n*. Odd = 2*n* + 1, with *n* a natural number.
5. Prove it: Theorem 1, even + odd = odd, with Lang’s proof. Then the learner proves odd + odd = even.
6. Prove it: Theorem 2. Expand (2*n* + 1)² = 2(2*n*² + 2*n*) + 1.
7. Name it: the corollary. If *a*² is even, then *a* is even. Show the logic with a two-row table: “if *a* is odd, then *a*² is odd”.
8. Name it: *d* divides *n* if *n* = *dk* for an integer *k*.
9. Practice: is 7 · 9 odd; the largest power of 2 that divides 48 (16); does 3 divide 51 (yes, 51 = 3 · 17).
10. Trap: “If *a*² is even, *a* can be odd” (false). Save Theorems 1 and 2 and the corollary.

Stretch (optional): congruence modulo 5, from the §4 exercises.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Says 3 divides 12 means 3 = 12*k* | “*d* divides *n*” means *n* = *d* × an integer. Write 12 as 3 × something. |
| Uses the same *n* for both numbers in a proof | The two numbers can be different. Use 2*n* for one and 2*k* + 1 for the other. |

### 1.6 Fractions (§5, 12 min)

**Goal:** compare, simplify, add and multiply fractions, and know why a denominator cannot be 0. **Needs:** 1.5.

1. Recall: does 4 divide 15?
2. Hook: is 2/4 of a bar more than 1/2 of the same bar? Make a guess.
3. Explore (fraction strips): cut a strip into smaller pieces. The shaded length stays the same.
4. Name it: a rational number is *m*/*n*, with *n* ≠ 0. A strip cut into 0 pieces has no pieces. A finite decimal is rational: 1.4 = 14/10. Each integer *m* is *m*/1.
5. Name it: the cross-multiplication rule. *m*/*n* = *r*/*s* if and only if *ms* = *rn*. Lang assumes this rule here. Lesson 1.8 proves it.
6. Name it: the cancellation rule, *am*/*an* = *m*/*n*, proved by cross-multiplication. A minus sign can move: −*m*/*n* = *m*/(−*n*). Positive and negative rational numbers.
7. Prove it: Theorem 3, every positive rational number has a lowest form. Use the greatest common divisor.
8. Explore: add 3/5 + 8/5 on one strip. Then add 2/3 + 1/4 on strips with a common denominator.
9. Name it: *m*/*n* + *r*/*s* = (*ms* + *rn*)/*ns*, and 0/*n* = 0. The addition rules of §2 stay true for rational numbers, so all proofs from 1.2 stay true.
10. Name it: (*m*/*n*)(*r*/*s*) = *mr*/*ns*. Cancel before you multiply. Powers: (*m*/*n*)ᵏ = *m*ᵏ/*n*ᵏ.
11. Explore (fraction strips): 40 g of a substance halves every 6 minutes. Find the mass after 18 minutes (40/2³ = 5 g).
12. Practice: is 6/8 = 9/12 (yes, 72 = 72); 18/24 in lowest form (3/4); 2/3 + 1/4 (11/12); (3/4)(8/9) (2/3, cancel first).
13. Trap: 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5 (false). Save the fraction rules.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7 | You added the tops and the bottoms. Put both fractions on the strip. Is 3/7 longer than 2/3? |
| Cross-multiplies 6 · 8 and 9 · 12 | Multiply across the diagonals: top-left by bottom-right, then bottom-left by top-right. |

### 1.7 Square root of 2 is not a fraction (§5, 10 min)

**Goal:** find decimals that come near √2, and follow Lang’s proof that no fraction is equal to √2. **Needs:** 1.5, 1.6.

1. Recall: (3/4)(8/9) = ?
2. Hook: a square has an area of 2. Guess the length of its side.
3. Explore (number line, zoom): 1² = 1 and 2² = 4, so the side is between 1 and 2. Then 1.4² = 1.96 and 1.5² = 2.25.
4. Explore: the learner selects the next digit. 1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164. Then 1.414² = 1.999396.
5. Name it: proof by contradiction. Use a daily example first. Suppose that it rained last night. Then the street is wet. The street is dry, so the supposition is false.
6. Prove it: Theorem 4. Write √2 as *m*/*n* in lowest form, then show that both *m* and *n* are even. Reasons: Theorem 3, the corollary, *m* = 2*k*.
7. Name it: a number that is not rational is irrational.
8. Practice: find the next decimal digit of √3 (1.73); stretch: do the same proof for √3, with “divisible by 3”.
9. Trap: “If you continue the decimals, you find √2 exactly” (false). Save Theorem 4.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Says 1.414² = 2 | A calculator can round. Multiply 1.414 × 1.414 in full. Is the result 1.999396 or 2? |
| Stops the proof at “*m* is even” | That shows only *m*. Put *m* = 2*k* back into *m*² = 2*n*². What does this show about *n*? |

### 1.8 Division and inverses (§6, 14 min)

**Goal:** use *a*⁻¹, use “if *ab* = 0, then *a* = 0 or *b* = 0”, and solve equations with fractions. **Needs:** 1.6.

1. Recall: is √2 rational?
2. Hook: find the number that you multiply by 2/3 to get exactly 1.
3. Explore (fraction strips): turn *m*/*n* upside down. Then (*m*/*n*)(*n*/*m*) = 1. Try 0: 0 times a number is always 0, never 1.
4. Name it: *a*⁻¹, the multiplicative inverse. Prove it: if *ab* = 1, then *b* = *a*⁻¹. Multiply the two sides by *a*⁻¹.
5. Prove it: if *ab* = 0, then *a* = 0 or *b* = 0. Use Lang’s proof with *a*⁻¹.
6. Use it: *x*(*x* − 3) = 0 gives *x* = 0 or *x* = 3.
7. Name it: for rational numbers *a* and *b* ≠ 0, write *a*/*b* for *b*⁻¹*a*.
8. Prove it: cross-multiplication for rational numbers. Use Lang’s proof with *b*⁻¹ and *d*⁻¹. The rule that 1.6 assumed is now proved.
9. Name it: the cancellation law. If *a* ≠ 0 and *ab* = *ac*, then *b* = *c*. Also *a*/*b* + *c*/*d* = (*ad* + *bc*)/*bd*. A denominator must not be 0.
10. Explore (balance): solve 3/(*x* + 2) = 4 by cross-multiplication. The answer is *x* = −5/4.
11. Explore: the formula *d* = *st* (distance = speed × time). A cyclist goes 6 km in 3/4 hour. Find the speed (8 km/h).
12. Practice: the inverse of −4/7 (−7/4); solve (2*x* − 1)/3 = 5 (*x* = 8).
13. Practice (word problem): you walk at 3 km/h and cycle at 15 km/h. In 2 hours you go 18 km. Find the cycle time (1 hour).
14. Trap: “*x*² = 3*x*. Divide by *x*, so *x* = 3 is the only solution” (false, *x* = 0 is also a solution). Save the inverse rules.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Inverse of −4/7 is 4/7 | The product must be +1. What is (−4/7)(4/7)? Keep the minus sign. |
| Divides both sides by *x* | If *x* = 0, you cannot divide by *x*. Move all terms to one side and factor. |

## Chapter 2: Linear equations

Chapter 2 uses one method, elimination, for two and three unknowns. The balance widget shows each step as the same change to two pans.

### 2.1 Two equations, two unknowns (§1, 12 min)

**Goal:** solve two linear equations by elimination, and identify a system with no solution. **Needs:** 1.8.

1. Recall: solve (2*x* − 1)/3 = 5.
2. Hook: a show sells 120 tickets. An adult ticket costs $8 and a child ticket costs $5. The total is $780. Guess the number of adult tickets.
3. Explore (balance): *x* + *y* = 10 and *x* − *y* = 4. Add the two equations. The *y* terms cancel, so 2*x* = 14.
4. Name it: elimination. Multiply the equations so that one letter has the same coefficient. Then add or subtract the equations.
5. Explore: 2*x* + *y* = 1 and 3*x* − 2*y* = 4. Multiply the first equation by 2 and add. Then *x* = 6/7 and *y* = −5/7.
6. Name it: a system can have no solution. Example: *x* + 2*y* = 3 and 2*x* + 4*y* = 5. Double the first equation to get 2*x* + 4*y* = 6.
7. Practice: *x* + *y* = 10, *x* − *y* = 4 (7, 3); the ticket problem (60 adult, 60 child); 2*x* + 3*y* = 12, *x* − *y* = 1 (3, 2).
8. Trap: “Two linear equations always have exactly one solution” (false). Save the elimination method.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Changes one side of an equation only | The balance tips. Do the same operation to both sides. |
| 3*x* − (−2*y*) becomes 3*x* − 2*y* | Subtract a minus number: −(−2*y*) = +2*y*. Use N4. |
| Does not check the answer | Put *x* and *y* into both equations. Both equations must be true. |

### 2.2 Three equations, three unknowns (§2, 10 min)

**Goal:** remove one letter at a time until one equation with one unknown stays. **Needs:** 2.1.

1. Recall: solve *x* + *y* = 5, *x* − *y* = 1 (3, 2).
2. Hook: three numbers *x*, *y*, *z* have a sum of 6. The learner gets two more clues and guesses the numbers.
3. Explore: *x* + *y* + *z* = 6, *x* − *y* + *z* = 2, 2*x* + *y* − *z* = 1. Subtract the second equation from the first: 2*y* = 4.
4. Explore: add the first and third equations: 3*x* + 2*y* = 7. Put in *y* = 2, so *x* = 1. Then *z* = 3.
5. Name it: remove the same letter from two pairs of equations. Then solve the two-unknown system. Check all three equations.
6. Practice: *x* + *y* + *z* = 9, *x* − *y* = 1, *y* − *z* = 1 (4, 3, 2).
7. Practice: ½*x* + *y* = 2, *x* − *y* + *z* = 1, *x* + *z* = 3 (0, 2, 3). First, multiply the first equation by 2 to remove the fraction.
8. Trap: “Remove *x* from one pair and *y* from the other pair” (false, two unknowns stay in each result). Save the method.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Removes different letters from the two pairs | Each result still has two unknowns. Remove the same letter from both pairs. |
| Keeps the fraction in ½*x* | Multiply the full equation by 2. Then all coefficients are integers. |

### 2.3 The general system (§1, exercises 9–11, 10 min)

Lang says a solution “usually” exists, and sends the reader to exercises 9 and 10 for the exact condition. This lesson follows those exercises.

**Goal:** solve *ax* + *by* = *u*, *cx* + *dy* = *v* with letters, and use *ad* − *bc* ≠ 0 as the condition for one solution. **Needs:** 2.1.

1. Recall: solve 2*x* + *y* = 1, 3*x* − 2*y* = 4 (*x* = 6/7, *y* = −5/7).
2. Hook: in 2.1, one system had no solution. Guess a test on the four coefficients that shows this before you solve.
3. Explore: a table of the 2.1 systems with *ad* − *bc*. For 2*x* + *y* = 1, 3*x* − 2*y* = 4: 2(−2) − 1 · 3 = −7, one solution. For *x* + 2*y* = 3, 2*x* + 4*y* = 5: 1 · 4 − 2 · 2 = 0, no solution.
4. Prove it: multiply the first equation by *d* and the second by *b*. Subtract. Then (*ad* − *bc*)*x* = *ud* − *bv*. Do the same for *y*: (*ad* − *bc*)*y* = *av* − *cu*.
5. Name it: if *ad* − *bc* ≠ 0, then *x* = (*ud* − *bv*)/(*ad* − *bc*) and *y* = (*av* − *cu*)/(*ad* − *bc*).
6. Prove it: put *x* and *y* back into the two equations. They are true (exercise 11).
7. Name it: if *u* = *v* = 0 and *ad* − *bc* ≠ 0, the only solution is *x* = 0, *y* = 0 (exercise 10).
8. Practice: solve 5*x* + 2*y* = 1, 3*x* + *y* = 2 with the formula. Here *ad* − *bc* = −1, so *x* = 3 and *y* = −7.
9. Note: Lang uses *ad* − *bc* again in his chapter on determinants.
10. Trap: “If *ad* − *bc* = 0, the system never has a solution” (false). *x* + *y* = 1 and 2*x* + 2*y* = 2 have many solutions. Save the general solution.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Uses *ad* + *bc* | Look at step 4. Subtract the two products: *ad* − *bc*. |
| Writes *x* = (*bv* − *ud*)/(*ad* − *bc*) | The order on top changes the sign. Repeat step 4 and compare. |

## Chapter 3: Real numbers

Chapter 3 fills the full line with numbers and adds three new tools: square roots, powers with any rational exponent, and inequalities. All rules from Chapter 1 stay true.

### 3.1 The real numbers (§1, 8 min)

**Goal:** know that the real numbers are all points on the line, and that the Chapter 1 rules apply to them. **Needs:** 1.7.

1. Recall: is √2 rational?
2. Hook: tap a point on the line at random. Guess if the point is a fraction.
3. Explore (number line, zoom): each point has a decimal expansion. Some expansions do not stop, for example 9.123145…
4. Name it: real numbers. Addition and multiplication obey the same rules. Each real number *a* ≠ 0 has an inverse.
5. Explore: the E/I system (E = even, I = odd). The learner completes the addition and multiplication tables. Only two objects obey the same rules.
6. Practice: (√2 + 1)(√2 − 1) = ? (1). Use (*a* + *b*)(*a* − *b*).
7. Trap: “The rules N1–N12 apply to integers only” (false).

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| (√2 + 1)(√2 − 1) = 3 | Use the formula *a*² − *b*². What is (√2)² − 1²? |

### 3.2 Positive numbers and square roots (§2, 12 min)

**Goal:** use POS 1 and POS 2, find both square roots, and use |*x*|. **Needs:** 3.1.

1. Recall: (√2 + 1)(√2 − 1) = ?
2. Hook: *x*² = 9. Guess the number of solutions.
3. Explore (number line, mirror): 3 and −3 have the same square.
4. Name it: POS 1 and POS 2. Prove it: 1 is positive. Suppose −1 is positive. Then (−1)(−1) = 1 is positive, which is a contradiction.
5. Prove it: if *x*² = *b*², then *x* = *b* or *x* = −*b*. Reasons: (*x* + *b*)(*x* − *b*) = 0 and the rule from 1.8.
6. Name it: √*a* is the positive root only. The solutions of *x*² = *a* are *x* = ±√*a*. Say “plus or minus root *a*”.
7. Name it: |*x*| = √(*x*²). On the line, |*x*| is the distance from *x* to 0.
8. Practice: *x*² = 7 (±√7); √((−4)²) (4); |*x* − 2| = 5 (7 or −3).
9. Trap: “√(*x*²) = *x* for all *x*” (false; if *x* = −4, the result is 4). Save POS 1, POS 2, √ and |*x*|.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| *x*² = 9 gives *x* = 3 only | Look at the mirror screen. Which other number has a square of 9? |
| \|*x* − 2\| = 5 gives *x* = 7 only | The distance from *x* to 2 is 5. Find the point 5 units to the left of 2. |

### 3.3 Simplify square roots (§2, 12 min)

**Goal:** remove a square root from a denominator or a numerator with (*x* + *y*)(*x* − *y*) = *x*² − *y*². **Needs:** 3.2.

1. Recall: (*a* + *b*)(*a* − *b*) = ?
2. Hook: compare 1/(3 + √2) and 0.25. Guess which number is larger.
3. Explore (area model): (3 + √2)(3 − √2) = 9 − 2 = 7. The two √2 strips cancel.
4. Name it: rationalise the denominator. Multiply the top and the bottom by 3 − √2. The result is (3 − √2)/7. Lang says this step is not useful in general. Use it only when it helps.
5. Name it: rationalise the numerator. (√(*x* + *h*) − √*x*)/*h* = 1/(√(*x* + *h*) + √*x*). Calculus uses this step.
6. Explore (balance): solve √(*x* + 5) = 1 + √*x*. Square the two sides: *x* + 5 = 1 + 2√*x* + *x*. Then √*x* = 2, so *x* = 4. Check: √9 = 3 = 1 + 2.
7. Explore: solve √(*x* + 5) = 1 − √*x*. The same steps give *x* = 4. The check fails: 3 ≠ 1 − 2. This equation has no solution.
8. Name it: if you square the two sides, you can get a false solution. Always check the answer in the original equation.
9. Practice: 1/(√5 − 2) (√5 + 2); 2/(1 + √3) (√3 − 1); solve √(*x* − 3) = 3 − √*x* (*x* = 4).
10. Trap: “√(*a* + *b*) = √*a* + √*b*” (false; √(9 + 16) = 5, not 7). Save the method.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Multiplies the bottom only | The value changes. Multiply the top and the bottom by the same number. |
| Multiplies by 3 + √2 | Use the opposite sign. Then the √2 terms cancel. |

### 3.4 Powers and roots (§3, 12 min)

**Goal:** use *n*-th roots, *a*⁰ = 1, negative powers and fractional powers. **Needs:** 1.4, 3.2.

1. Recall: 2³ · 2⁴ = ?
2. Hook: 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2. Continue the pattern. Guess 2⁰ and 2⁻¹.
3. Explore (halving strip): each step to the left divides by 2.
4. Name it: the *n*-th root of *a* > 0 is the positive *r* with *r*ⁿ = *a*. Write *a*¹ᐟⁿ. Theorem 1: (*ab*)¹ᐟⁿ = *a*¹ᐟⁿ*b*¹ᐟⁿ.
5. Prove it: *a*⁰ = 1. Reason: *a* = *a*⁰⁺¹ = *a*⁰*a* (POW 1). Then multiply by *a*⁻¹.
6. Prove it: *a*⁻ˣ = 1/*a*ˣ. Reason: *a*ˣ*a*⁻ˣ = *a*⁰ = 1.
7. Name it: *a*ᵐᐟⁿ = (*a*¹ᐟⁿ)ᵐ. Take the root first, then the power. Lang postpones irrational exponents, for example 2 to the power √2, to a later course.
8. Practice: 27²ᐟ³ (9); 16⁻¹ᐟ² (1/4); (4/9)³ᐟ² (8/27); 5⁰ (1).
9. Trap: “2⁻³ is a negative number” (false, 2⁻³ = 1/8). Save POW 1–POW 3.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| *a*⁰ = 0 | Look at the pattern: 4, 2, … Each step divides by 2. What comes after 2? |
| 27²ᐟ³ = 18 | The exponent is not a multiplier. Find the cube root of 27 first. Then square it. |

### 3.5 Inequalities (§4, 12 min)

**Goal:** use IN 1–IN 3, solve linear inequalities, and write the answer as an interval. **Needs:** 3.2.

1. Recall: 16⁻¹ᐟ² = ?
2. Hook: 1 < 3. Multiply the two sides by −2. Is −2 < −6 true? Make a guess.
3. Explore (number line): multiplication by −1 mirrors the two points across 0. Their order changes.
4. Name it: *a* > *b* means *a* − *b* > 0. IN 1, IN 2 and IN 3.
5. Prove it: IN 1. Reasons: the definition of >, then POS 1.
6. Name it: intervals. A filled dot includes an endpoint. An empty dot excludes it. An infinite interval, for example x > 7, has one endpoint only.
7. Practice: −2*x* + 1 < 5 (*x* > −2); 3*x* + 2 < 1 (*x* < −1/3).
8. Practice: (2*x* − 1)/(*x* + 2) < 1. Examine the cases *x* > −2 and *x* < −2 separately. The answer is −2 < *x* < 3.
9. Trap: “If you multiply the two sides by a number, the inequality stays the same” (false). Save IN 1–IN 3.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| −2*x* < 4 gives *x* < −2 | You divided by −2. Look at the mirror screen. What happens to the inequality sign? |
| Multiplies by *x* + 2 without cases | The sign of *x* + 2 is not known. Examine *x* + 2 > 0 and *x* + 2 < 0 separately. |

### 3.6 More rules for inequalities (§4, exercises 2–8 and 25–32, 12 min)

Lang states IN 1–IN 3, then says that other properties come easily from them and leaves them as exercises. He adds that these properties are used constantly. This lesson proves them.

**Goal:** prove the inequality rules that Lang leaves to the reader, and solve *x*² < *k* and *x*² > *k*. **Needs:** 3.5.

1. Recall: solve −2*x* + 1 < 5.
2. Hook: 2 < 3 and 4 < 5, so 2 · 4 < 3 · 5. Also −3 < −2 and −5 < −4. Guess: is (−3)(−5) < (−2)(−4)?
3. Prove it: if *a* < *b*, then *a* + *c* < *b* + *c*. Reason: (*b* + *c*) − (*a* + *c*) = *b* − *a* > 0.
4. Prove it: if 0 < *a* < *b*, *c* < *d* and *c* > 0, then *ac* < *bd*. Reasons: *ac* < *bc* (IN 2), *bc* < *bd* (IN 2), then IN 1.
5. Prove it: if *a* < *b* < 0 and *c* < *d* < 0, then *ac* > *bd*. This answers the hook: 15 > 8.
6. Prove it: if 0 < *x* < *y*, then 1/*y* < 1/*x*. Multiply the two sides by 1/(*xy*), which is positive.
7. Name it: cross-multiplication of inequalities. If *b* > 0 and *d* > 0, then *a*/*b* < *c*/*d* if and only if *ad* < *bc*.
8. Explore (square tiles): if 0 < *a* < *b*, a square of side *a* fits inside a square of side *b*. So *a*² < *b*², then *a*³ < *b*³. Lang notes that a full proof for all *n* needs induction, a later chapter. Then *a*¹ᐟⁿ < *b*¹ᐟⁿ.
9. Explore (fraction strips): if *b*, *d* > 0 and *a*/*b* < *c*/*d*, then (*a* + *c*)/(*b* + *d*) is between the two. Example: 1/3 < 2/5 < 1/2.
10. Practice: *x*² < 4 (−2 < *x* < 2); *x*² > 3 (*x* > √3 or *x* < −√3).
11. Trap: “If *a* < *b*, then *a*² < *b*²” (false: −3 < 1, but 9 > 1). Save the rules.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| *x*² < 4 gives *x* < 2 only | Try *x* = −5. Is (−5)² < 4? Find the left endpoint too. |
| Uses *a*² < *b*² with negative *a* | The rule needs 0 < *a*. Look at the trap example. |

## Chapter 4: Quadratic equations

Chapter 4 solves *ax*² + *bx* + *c* = 0. Lesson 4.1 makes the method visible with square tiles. Lesson 4.2 turns the same steps into one formula.

### 4.1 Complete the square (12 min)

**Goal:** change *x*² + *bx* into a perfect square, then solve with square roots. **Needs:** 1.4, 3.2.

1. Recall: solve *x*² = 7.
2. Hook (square tiles): *x*² + 6*x* is a square and two strips. Guess the missing piece that makes a full square.
3. Explore: drag the 3 × 3 corner into the gap. Then *x*² + 6*x* + 9 = (*x* + 3)².
4. Name it: to complete *x*² + *bx*, add (*b*/2)².
5. Explore: solve *x*² + 6*x* = 7. Add 9 to the two sides: (*x* + 3)² = 16. Then *x* + 3 = ±4, so *x* = 1 or *x* = −7.
6. Explore: *x*² − 3*x* + 1 = 0 gives (*x* − 3/2)² = 5/4. Then *x* = 3/2 ± √5/2.
7. Name it: if the right side is negative, there is no real solution. Example: *x*² + 4*x* + 5 = 0 gives (*x* + 2)² = −1.
8. Explore: if *x*² has a coefficient, divide by it first. 2*x*² − 3*x* − 5 = 0 gives (*x* − 3/4)² = 49/16. Then *x* = 5/2 or *x* = −1.
9. Practice: *x*² − 8*x* = 9 (9 or −1); *x*² + 2*x* − 4 = 0 (−1 ± √5).
10. Trap: “*x*² = 16 gives *x* = 4 only” (false). Save the method.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| Adds 9 to the left side only | The balance tips. Add 9 to the two sides. |
| Adds *b*² = 36 | Look at the tiles. The corner is 3 × 3. Use (*b*/2)². |
| Writes *x* + 3 = 4 only | A square of 16 has two roots. Use ±4. |

### 4.2 The quadratic formula (12 min)

**Goal:** use *x* = (−*b* ± √(*b*² − 4*ac*))/2*a*, and use *b*² − 4*ac* to find the number of solutions. **Needs:** 4.1.

1. Recall: complete the square for *x*² + 2*x*.
2. Hook: the steps in 4.1 are the same for each equation. Guess if one formula can do all equations.
3. Prove it: Lang’s derivation with *a*, *b* and *c*. The learner supplies each reason: divide by *a*, add *b*²/4*a*², take the square root.
4. Name it: the quadratic formula. Read it aloud: “*x* equals minus *b*, plus or minus root of *b* squared minus 4*ac*, over 2*a*”.
5. Explore (number line with a slider for *c*): if *b*² − 4*ac* > 0, there are two solutions. If it is 0, there is one. If it is negative, there are none.
6. Practice: 2*x*² + 3*x* − 2 = 0 (1/2, −2); *x*² − 6*x* + 9 = 0 (3); *x*² + 2*x* + 5 = 0 (no real solution).
7. Practice: 3*x*² − 2*x* + 1 = 0. Calculate *b*² − 4*ac* first (−8, no real solution).
8. Trap: “If *b* is negative, −*b* is negative” (false). Save the formula. Lang notes that in a larger number system, the complex numbers, the formula gives solutions in all cases. A later chapter covers this.

| Likely mistake | Reply |
| --- | --- |
| For *b* = −6, writes −*b* = −6 | Use N4: −(−6) = 6. |
| For *b* = −6, writes *b*² = −36 | (−6)² = (−6)(−6). Use the rule from 1.3. |
| Divides only √(…) by 2*a* | The full top, −*b* ± √(…), is over 2*a*. |
